Disaster recovery (DR): what it means

The written plan and standby infrastructure that bring a company's IT back after a disaster, measured by two numbers: RTO and RPO.

Disaster recovery (DR) is the written plan, together with data copies and standby infrastructure, by which a company restarts its IT systems and recovers its data after a major incident — fire, flood, ransomware, serious hardware failure — within two targets set in advance: RTO, how long a system may stay down, and RPO, how much data, measured in time, it can afford to lose.

Run this mental stress test: your server died overnight. How long until you can invoice again, and how much data is gone for good? Disaster recovery turns the answer from "no idea, hopefully soon" into two numbers the business has accepted. Sensible sizing starts on the business side: one hour of downtime costs the company a known amount, and the worthwhile investment follows from there. For a smaller firm, the reasonable package is tested off-site backup, rebuild documentation and one annual drill.

RTO/RPO definitions follow NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1; the NIS2 and GDPR references were checked on 18 November 2026.

Why it matters for your business

Survival by design, not luck

A plan is the difference between a one-day incident and a company that can no longer invoice: you know in advance what restarts first and from which copy.

Investment sized to real money

RTO and RPO tie IT spending to the actual cost of downtime — you pay for neither paranoia nor false economy on the firm's main artery.

Procedural calm on the worst day

When everything is burning, the team executes written, rehearsed steps — instead of improvising on adrenaline with passwords nobody can find.

RTO and RPO: the two numbers of the plan

NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1 defines RPO as the point in time to which data must be recovered after an outage, and RTO as the overall length of time an information system's components can be in the recovery phase before negatively impacting the organization's mission. In short: RPO measures how old the data is when you restart, RTO measures how long the restart takes.

Example: a nightly backup taken at 02:00 means an RPO of up to about 24 hours, because a failure at 01:59 loses the whole day. Restoring onto a new server usually takes hours or days; that is the RTO. Continuous replication plus automatic switchover (see failover) pulls both numbers toward minutes, at a much higher cost.

Targets are set per system, starting from a business impact analysis (BIA): what an hour of downtime costs for each system. An ERP may demand an RTO of hours and an RPO of 15 minutes, while a brochure site can wait days and lose a day of data.

What a disaster recovery plan contains

The plan starts with an inventory of critical systems and the dependencies between them (without DNS or the mail account, a restored system will not start), then the BIA and the RTO/RPO targets per system.

Next come the copies. The 3-2-1 rule from CISA's "Data Backup Options" (2012) asks for three copies of important data, on two different media types, with one stored offsite. The rest of the backup topic lives on the backup page.

Then the place you restore to. A cold site is space and hardware without data, a warm site has systems installed and data still to restore, and a hot site is a running, synchronised replica. The hotter it is, the lower the RTO and the higher the cost.

The last part is the runbook: who does what, in which order and with which access. Passwords and contacts are kept outside the systems that can fail, and the plan covers communication with customers and suppliers.

Disaster recovery, backup, failover and business continuity

A backup is the copy of the data. Failover is the automatic switch of a component to its standby. Disaster recovery is rebuilding IT after the loss of a system or a site, using the copies and the standby.

Business continuity is wider: the whole company keeps operating, meaning people, premises, suppliers and communication. Disaster recovery is its IT part. The management standard for business continuity is ISO 22301.

In short, a backup is one piece of the plan, and the plan is one piece of continuity.

Testing a disaster recovery plan

An untested plan is a hypothesis. A test has three levels: a tabletop exercise (the team walks through the runbook step by step), a trial restore of one system onto clean infrastructure, and a full, timed exercise. At the end you compare the RTO and RPO achieved with the targets.

Nearly every test uncovers something: an incomplete backup, an unknown password, a forgotten dependency. Northdan recommends a test at least once a year and after every major system change. That frequency is our recommendation, not a requirement of any standard or law.

GDPR art. 32(1)(d) asks for "a process for regularly testing, assessing and evaluating the effectiveness of technical and organisational measures", without fixing a frequency.

Disaster recovery under NIS2 and GDPR

The NIS2 Directive (EU) 2022/2555 lists, in art. 21(2)(c), business continuity, such as backup management and disaster recovery, and crisis management among the minimum risk-management measures for essential and important entities. Romania transposed it as OUG 155/2024, where art. 13 lit. h) carries the same measure; scope, deadlines and penalties are on the OUG 155/2024 page.

Outside NIS2, GDPR art. 32(1)(c) requires "the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data in a timely manner in the event of a physical or technical incident". It applies to any company that processes personal data. A customer covered by NIS2 may also ask its suppliers for the same assurances, through supply-chain security.

Frequently asked questions

We already have backups — isn't that the same as disaster recovery?⌄

No: a backup is the copy of the data, while disaster recovery is the restart plan. The plan says where you restore when the server burned along with the room, who executes, in what order systems restart and how the company works meanwhile. If you cannot describe the day after in writing, you own a backup, not a plan.

What do RTO and RPO mean for my company in practice?⌄

RTO is how long you can afford to be down, and RPO is how much data, measured in time, you can afford to lose. They are set per system: an ERP may demand hours and 15 minutes, a brochure site days and a full day. Small numbers get exponentially expensive, so start from the cost of an hour of downtime.

How often should a disaster recovery plan be tested?⌄

Northdan recommends at least once a year, with a real, timed restore onto clean infrastructure, plus after every major system change. No law or standard cited here fixes that frequency. Nearly every test uncovers something: an incomplete backup, an unknown password, a forgotten dependency.

What is the difference between disaster recovery and business continuity?⌄

Disaster recovery brings IT back after a major incident, while business continuity keeps the whole company running: people, premises, suppliers, communication. DR is the IT part of continuity. You can have a good DR plan and still not know where the team works if the office is inaccessible.

What should a disaster recovery plan contain?⌄

A list of critical systems with an RTO and RPO for each, copies following the 3-2-1 rule, the restore location (cold, warm or hot), a runbook with roles and start-up order, contacts and access kept outside the systems that can fail, and a schedule of tests.

Is disaster recovery required by law?⌄

For essential and important entities under NIS2, yes: Directive (EU) 2022/2555 art. 21(2)(c) lists disaster recovery among the minimum measures, and Romania's OUG 155/2024 art. 13 lit. h) repeats it. For any company processing personal data, GDPR art. 32(1)(c) requires the ability to restore data in a timely manner after an incident.
