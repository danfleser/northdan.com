What is single sign-on (SSO)?

Single sign-on (SSO) is one authentication: you sign in once with the company account and enter every connected application without further passwords.

Single sign-on (SSO) is the method by which you authenticate once, at an identity provider (IdP) such as Microsoft Entra ID or Google Workspace, and then enter several applications without typing your password into each one. The US standard NIST SP 800-63-4 (final version, 31.07.2025) defines it as one account and its authenticators used for several applications, generally through a federation protocol. In practice that protocol is SAML 2.0 or OpenID Connect: the application never sees your password, it receives a signed confirmation from the identity provider. SSO does not mean the same password copied into every application (that is password synchronization) and it does not mean OAuth, which grants access rather than proving who you are (the glossary page on OAuth explains the difference).

For a company, the real gains are three: one place to enforce 2FA and access policies, one log of who authenticated where and when, and zero per-app passwords to remember or reset. A fourth gain, departures, has a limit that is rarely mentioned: deactivating the account stops new sign-ins in every application, but sessions that are already open stay valid until they expire (see below). The honest cost: the central account becomes a single point of failure, so 2FA on it is not optional, and some SaaS vendors keep SSO in expensive plans.

Why it matters for your business

✓Departures closed from a single place

One deactivation blocks new sign-ins to every connected application; with deprovisioning, the accounts inside the applications disappear too, so there is no hunt for forgotten accounts.

✓Defense concentrated in a single point

2FA, access policies and monitoring are configured once, at the identity provider, instead of thirty applications guarded unevenly.

✓Zero app passwords, zero resets

People sign in with the account they already have — a small daily productivity gain, and an IT team freed from “forgot my password” tickets.

How SSO works

Every SSO involves three actors: the user, the application (in standards language, the service provider or relying party) and the identity provider (IdP). The application does not hold the password; it trusts the IdP.

The flow has four steps. You open the application, which redirects you to the IdP. The IdP checks your session or asks for your password and second factor. It then sends back a signed confirmation: a SAML assertion (XML) or an OpenID Connect ID Token (JWT). Finally, the application opens its own session for you. The last step matters at departures: the application's session belongs to the application, not to the IdP.

SAML 2.0 and OpenID Connect: the difference

SAML 2.0 is an OASIS Standard approved on 01.03.2005 (also adopted as ITU-T X.1141). It carries XML assertions about authentication, attributes and authorization, and is widely established in enterprise applications; large customers, procurement rules and older IdPs that lack OIDC often require it.

OpenID Connect 1.0 (OIDC) became final on 26.02.2014 and was published as ISO/IEC 26131:2024 on 01.10.2024. It is an identity layer built on top of OAuth 2.0: the application receives an ID Token in JWT format, over JSON and REST. It works naturally in mobile and single-page applications.

Microsoft's decision guide (Microsoft Learn, 22.06.2026) recommends OIDC as the default for new applications, and SAML when the customer, procurement or a legacy IdP requires it. With mixed customers, the application supports both. OAuth 2.0 alone is an authorization protocol, not an authentication one; sign-in on top of it is OIDC.

Examples of SSO

The most common: the company Microsoft Entra ID or Google Workspace account, with which an employee enters the CRM, accounting, HR and project tools that support SAML or OIDC. On public websites, the “Continue with Google” or “Continue with Microsoft” button is also SSO, usually through OIDC. On the internal network, Windows sign-in with Active Directory (Kerberos) gives the same effect for internal resources.

When an employee leaves: what SSO stops and what it does not

Deactivating the central account blocks new sign-ins. Microsoft's documentation (Microsoft Learn, 19.06.2026) notes, however, that an access token lasts one hour by default, and the session cookie each application issues cannot be revoked directly by Entra ID. For a real stop: disable the account, revoke the sessions and, where possible, use automatic deprovisioning.

Deprovisioning runs through SCIM (RFC 7643 and RFC 7644, IETF, September 2015): the IdP deactivates the account inside the application as well. In Entra, the sync cycle runs every 20-40 minutes. Accounts created outside SSO, such as a local login or an API key, stay active, so they go on the manual offboarding checklist.

What to check with the vendor and in the requirements

The first criterion when buying a SaaS application: does it offer SSO in the plan you are buying? Many vendors put it only in enterprise plans, a practice called the “SSO tax”. Ask before you get attached to the product, then negotiate or look for an alternative.

The second: the single point of failure. A compromised central account opens everything, so require phishing-resistant 2FA (passkeys or hardware keys), conditional access and centrally revocable sessions. If the IdP goes down, nobody gets in; know that in advance.

For a custom-built application, the requirement is one simple line in the specification: “sign-in through our Microsoft/Google account (OIDC; SAML if a customer requires it), plus SCIM deprovisioning”.

Frequently asked questions

We are a small company — isn’t SSO for corporations?

No, it is no longer only for corporations. If you use Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, the identity provider already exists, and connecting compatible SaaS applications is configuration, not a project. The pragmatic start: new applications connect through SSO from day one, existing ones at renewal.

What happens if an employee’s central account is compromised?

Precisely the scenario SSO is built for — with mandatory 2FA (ideally passkeys or hardware keys, which resist phishing) and policies: conditional access by location and device, centrally revocable sessions, alerts on abnormal behavior. And when an incident does occur, the architecture helps the response: one place to block, one log to investigate — versus the impossible question “which of the 30 applications did they get into?”. The single point is more defensible than distributed chaos — provided it is actually defended.

The application we want requires the enterprise plan for SSO — how do we handle it?

That is the “SSO tax” — irritating and widespread: a basic security feature packaged on the expensive floor. Options: negotiate (many vendors yield to a firm request, especially on annual contracts), pick the alternative offering decent SSO in normal tiers (a legitimate procurement criterion), or consciously accept the exception — with a unique password from the password manager and the account added manually to the offboarding checklist. What you don’t do: abandon SSO everywhere because three vendors sell it dearly.

What is the difference between SSO and SAML?

SSO is the outcome: you authenticate once and enter several applications. SAML 2.0 is one of the protocols that carries that authentication between the identity provider and the application, alongside OpenID Connect. You can have SSO through SAML, through OIDC or through both.

Does SSO replace two-factor authentication?

No. SSO moves authentication to a single place, so 2FA also applies there, once, for all applications. The central account needs 2FA, ideally phishing-resistant: passkeys or hardware keys. Without 2FA, SSO would mean a single password that opens everything.

What is the difference between SSO and Active Directory?

Active Directory (or Microsoft Entra ID) is the directory and identity provider: accounts and groups are held there. SSO is the way applications accept that identity, through Kerberos on the internal network or through SAML and OpenID Connect for cloud applications.

SSO or a password manager?

They complement each other. SSO covers applications that support SAML or OpenID Connect; the password manager covers applications without SSO, with unique generated passwords. Those left outside SSO go on the manual offboarding checklist.
