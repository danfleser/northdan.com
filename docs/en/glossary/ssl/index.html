What is an SSL Certificate?

SSL is the old name of the TLS protocol, and an SSL certificate is the file, signed by a certificate authority, that proves a website's identity and switches on the encrypted HTTPS connection.

SSL (Secure Sockets Layer) is the protocol that encrypts the connection between a browser and a website, created by Netscape in the 1990s and replaced today by TLS (Transport Layer Security); the name stayed in use, so an "SSL certificate" is in fact a TLS certificate, signed by a certificate authority, proving that the site controls the domain and starting the HTTPS connection. Every SSL version is prohibited (SSL 3.0 since 2015, RFC 7568), TLS 1.0 and 1.1 have been retired since 2021 (RFC 8996), and today TLS 1.2 and 1.3 are used. Since 15 March 2026 a public certificate is valid for 200 days at most, and the CA/Browser Forum rule drops to 100 days (15 March 2027) and 47 days (15 March 2029).

For a company, what matters is the commercial and administrative side. The real cost is zero for the standard need: Let's Encrypt, the free authority run by the nonprofit ISRG, serves more than 700 million websites (per letsencrypt.org, on 18 November 2026) and comes with automatic renewal. Paid certificates make sense only for organization validation (OV/EV), which also attests the legal entity. The encryption is identical either way and only what is attested differs, so selling small firms expensive certificates "for better security" is billing on misunderstanding.

Why it matters for your business

✓Visitor trust kept intact

A valid connection goes unnoticed, while the red expired-certificate warning scares away even loyal customers; proper administration prevents exactly that scene.

✓Full encryption at zero cost

Modern free certificates provide the same encryption as any paid option — spend the budget on correct configuration, not expensive paperwork.

✓Prerequisite for everything that matters

Payments, GDPR-compliant forms, Google rankings, modern APIs — all assume working HTTPS; a healthy certificate is the invisible infrastructure of every conversion.

How an SSL certificate works

The certificate contains the domain name it was issued for, the server's public key, the certificate authority (CA) that signed it, and the start and end dates of its validity. The browser checks three things: that the signature leads, through the certificate chain, to a root authority the browser trusts (the server must send the intermediate certificate too), that the name in the certificate matches the address opened, and that today's date falls inside the validity period.

If the checks pass, the browser and the server use the key exchange to set up one-time session keys, and everything that travels between them is encrypted. A network eavesdropper sees only unreadable data.

SSL and TLS: the protocol versions

SSL 2.0 has been prohibited since March 2011 (RFC 6176), and SSL 3.0 "MUST NOT be used" since June 2015 (RFC 7568). TLS 1.0 (RFC 2246, January 1999) and TLS 1.1 (RFC 4346) were deprecated in March 2021 (RFC 8996). TLS 1.2 (RFC 5246, 2008) is still in use, and TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446, August 2018) is the current version.

In practice: a server should accept only TLS 1.2 and 1.3. The name "SSL" stayed on invoices and in hosting panels, but the certificate behind it is the same.

Types of SSL certificates

DV (domain validation) only confirms that whoever requests it controls the domain. It is free, issued in minutes and enough for almost any company site. OV and EV (organization validation and extended validation) also attest the legal entity behind the domain; they are paid and make sense when a finance or corporate partner asks for them in writing.

A wildcard certificate (*.company.com) covers all subdomains one level deep, and a multi-domain (SAN) certificate lists several names. Let's Encrypt issues wildcards for free too, but only through DNS validation (DNS-01).

How long an SSL certificate is valid

The maximum validity of a public TLS certificate shrinks on a schedule set by the CA/Browser Forum (ballot SC-081v3, passed on 11 April 2025 with the votes of Apple, Google, Microsoft and Mozilla): 398 days until 14 March 2026, 200 days from 15 March 2026, 100 days from 15 March 2027 and 47 days from 15 March 2029.

Let's Encrypt issues 90-day certificates today. An optional 45-day profile has been available since 13 May 2026, and the default validity drops to 64 days on 10 February 2027 and to 45 days on 16 February 2028.

The consequence: a paid certificate bought "for 2 years" is really a subscription re-issued and reinstalled at least every 200 days. Renewal has to be automatic (the ACME protocol) and monitored independently. Checked on 18 November 2026 against Baseline Requirements v2.3.1 and letsencrypt.org.

The administration mistakes that take a site down

Expiry is the most expensive: an expired certificate shows every visitor a full-screen warning, and the usual cause is an automatic renewal that failed silently after a hosting or DNS change. Next come incomplete coverage (a missing www or subdomain), mixed content (resources loaded over http inside https pages) and an incomplete chain (the intermediate certificate is missing).

The free Qualys SSL Labs test grades the configuration in a minute: A is the standard; below B, there is server work to do, not a pricier certificate to buy.

Frequently asked questions

How much does an SSL certificate cost, and what do you get extra with a paid one?

For an ordinary company site, nothing: the free certificate (DV, from Let's Encrypt) encrypts exactly like a paid one. What differs is what is attested: paid OV/EV also confirm the legal organization, relevant in finance or corporate where partners require it, and come with support. Free with automatic renewal is the correct choice.

How do we prevent a surprise certificate expiry?

Three cheap layers: automatic renewal confirmed to have run successfully at least once, independent monitoring with an alert before expiry, and a named owner, because hosting or DNS changes break renewal first. Let's Encrypt has not sent expiry emails since 4 June 2025, so a separate monitor is mandatory.

The browser says the connection is not secure even though we have a certificate — what can it be?

The usual suspects: the certificate expired after a failed renewal, wrong coverage (issued for www but accessed without it, or a subdomain missing), mixed content (an https page loading resources over http, so the browser marks the page as not secure) or an unsent certificate chain. An online SSL test finds them in a minute.

What is the difference between SSL and TLS?

TLS is the successor of SSL: its first version, TLS 1.0, appeared in January 1999 (RFC 2246). Every SSL version is prohibited (RFC 6176 and RFC 7568), so servers use TLS 1.2 and 1.3. The certificate is the same object; "SSL" survives only as the commercial name.

How often is an SSL certificate renewed?

At most every 200 days from 15 March 2026, every 100 days from 15 March 2027 and every 47 days from 15 March 2029 (CA/Browser Forum). Let's Encrypt issues 90-day certificates and recommends automatic renewal when 30 days remain, around day 60.

Can a site with an SSL certificate still be fake?

Yes: a DV certificate only proves control of the domain, not who runs the site, and phishing sites get free certificates in minutes. That is why Chrome 117 (September 2023) replaced the padlock with a neutral settings icon. Check the domain name, not the icon.
