What is a wireframe?

A plain sketch of a website or app screen, without colours or images: which elements it has, where they sit and in what order.

A wireframe (literally a frame made of wire; also called a page schematic or screen blueprint) is a simplified sketch, usually in black and white, of a web page or app screen that shows which elements it contains, where they sit and in what order, without colours, typefaces or final images. The term comes from 3D graphics, where a wireframe model represents an object by its edges alone. On the approved structure comes the mockup (the visual design, static), then the prototype (linked screens you can click through) and only then development.

The visual poverty is the method, not an omission: on a grey sketch the discussion stays on structure (the price is missing, the main action is buried), while on a polished design feedback slides towards shades of blue and opinions about photography. Moving a block on a sketch takes a minute; after development the same change costs days.

Why it matters for your business

✓Structure corrected while it costs a minute

Moving a block or removing a step on a sketch is free; the same corrections on a finished design or inside code cost days and a certain amount of pride.

✓Feedback on substance, not taste

Black and white keeps the early discussion on what matters — hierarchy, content, flows — rather than on the shades that have their proper turn later.

✓A shared language between business and team

A sketch everybody understands aligns your vision with their implementation before any cost is incurred, including when you draw it yourself on paper.

What a wireframe contains

Blocks for the logo, menu, headings and text (real or placeholder), images (usually a rectangle with an X), the main button and forms. Beyond those, a wireframe fixes the hierarchy (what is seen first), the flow between screens and annotations written beside the sketch that explain behaviour: what happens on tap, which fields are required. The states most often missing are the error, the empty list and loading. The mobile version is sketched separately, because the order of blocks may differ.

Examples by screen type: a home page (menu, main message, sections, footer), a shop product page (gallery, price, stock, cart button), a booking form or an internal dashboard (filters, tables, charts).

Wireframe types by fidelity

Low fidelity: a sketch on paper or a whiteboard, only rectangles and labels. It takes minutes and is good for comparing variants. Mid fidelity: the sketch in a digital tool, with annotations and flows between screens; this is where the structure is usually approved. High fidelity: real content, typography and image sizes, shades of grey but no brand colours; it can start to look like an early mockup.

Real content matters, because placeholder text hides problems. A real headline can be far longer than the space left for it, and a section planned for several benefits can end up nearly empty once the real ones are written. A team can jump straight to high fidelity when a design system or a validated template already exists; then the template itself plays the role of the wireframe.

Wireframe, mockup and prototype: the difference

The three stages answer three questions. Wireframe: what and where? Mockup: how does it look? Prototype: how does it flow?

The wireframe shows structure and hierarchy but not colours, final typefaces or photographs; it is where you approve which information and actions exist and where they sit. The usual format is a sketch or a Figma file in shades of grey. The mockup shows the final design, static, on desktop and phone; it does not show movement or where each button leads; it is where you approve the look. The prototype links the screens into a link you can click through, so you can see how a user moves. Details on prototype types and user testing are on the glossary page about the prototype.

In practice the borders blur: a high-fidelity wireframe resembles an early mockup, and in Figma screens can be turned into a clickable prototype.

Wireframe tools

Paper and a whiteboard are the fastest option and work well in a meeting. Balsamiq is made for low-fidelity wireframes, with a hand-drawn look. Figma keeps the wireframe, mockup and prototype in one file; the client gets a link and comments directly on the element. Penpot is an open-source alternative (MPL-2.0 licence). Excalidraw is an open-source online whiteboard (MIT licence) with a hand-drawn style. Miro is a collaborative whiteboard, useful for flows.

AI tools can generate a first sketch from a text description. What is missing for your business (stock, price, the approval step) still has to be checked by a person who knows it.

What to check on a wireframe as the client

On a wireframe you do not comment on colours; you check that the important information and actions are present and ranked. "For us, stock must be visible straight away" costs a minute to fix now and days later. Your own paper sketch, photographed, communicates a screen need more precisely than two pages of description, and good teams welcome it.

The presence of this stage is the sign of a healthy process at a supplier: a project that jumps straight to a polished design saves days at the start and pays for them in rework. On a custom site, structure and wireframes usually take the first 1-2 weeks of 7-8, and our web design service delivers one wireframe per page type, in Figma.

Frequently asked questions

What feedback is useful on a wireframe, and what should wait?

Now, the structural questions: are the information and actions that matter to the business present, does the hierarchy reflect real priorities, does the flow contain unnecessary or missing steps, and is any screen or state absent, such as what a user sees on an error or an empty list. Later, on the visual design: colours, typefaces, imagery, tone. Saying you dislike how it looks at wireframe stage is like rejecting an architectural plan over the colour of the drawing.

Wireframe, mockup, prototype — a short recap of the differences?

Three answers to three questions. The wireframe answers what and where, as a black-and-white structural sketch. The mockup answers how it looks, as finished static visual design applied to the approved structure. The prototype answers how it flows, with screens linked interactively and navigable for testing on users before development starts. On small projects the stages compress, but the questions stay the same and stay in that order.

Small project, small budget — can we skip wireframes?

You can skip the formal deliverable sometimes; you cannot skip the stage. At any size, structure gets decided before aesthetics, even if the wireframe is an hour’s sketch on a whiteboard photographed at the end of a meeting. The cost of skipping shows up in rework, since a finished design rejected for structural reasons is paid for twice. One honest nuance: on template-based sites the proven template is itself a market-validated wireframe, so the discussion legitimately moves to choosing and adapting it.

Does a wireframe belong to UX or UI?

To UX: the wireframe decides structure, hierarchy and flow, meaning how a person gets to what they are looking for. UI (colours, typefaces, visual components) appears only on the mockup. In small teams the same person does both stages, but the approvals stay separate.

Who makes the wireframe, and can I make it myself?

Usually the supplier's UX designer or business analyst; on small projects, the web designer. You can sketch one yourself on paper or in Excalidraw, with the sections and order you want. It does not replace the team's wireframe, but it tells them exactly what you want.

Can an AI tool make the wireframe instead of the designer?

It can generate a first version from a description, useful as a starting point. It does not know what your customers ask on the phone, what data your firm holds or which approval step exists internally. Those get checked on the sketch by a person who knows the business.
