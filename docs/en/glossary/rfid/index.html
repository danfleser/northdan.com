What is RFID (radio-frequency identification) and how does it work

For a business, RFID means stock, equipment and packaging read in bulk without scanning item by item, and movements recorded automatically in the warehouse system.

RFID (Radio-Frequency Identification) is the technology by which a reader automatically recognises, by radio waves, a tag with a chip and antenna attached to an object, with no physical contact and no line of sight, and unlike a barcode it can read many tags at once. A system has three parts: the tag (the chip holds a unique identifier, the antenna transmits it), the reader (handheld, or a fixed gate with antennas) and the software that links the identifier to the product, pallet or piece of equipment it sits on. A passive tag has no battery and takes power from the reader's radio field; an active tag has its own battery and can be read from further away, but costs more.

Applications reach beyond the warehouse: tracking tools and equipment on construction sites, returnable containers, textiles in industrial laundries, and access control. The business decision is pure arithmetic: a tag costs more than a printed barcode, so RFID is justified where the value of visibility (fast stocktakes, fewer losses, no forgotten scans) exceeds the cost per tag, plus the readers and the integration with the ERP.

Why it matters for your business

✓Stocktakes without item-by-item scanning

Bulk reading without handling the goods makes counting cheaper and so more frequent — recorded stock and physical stock stop being two different realities.

✓Movements recorded without human discipline

RFID gates automatically note what enters and what leaves — removing the dependence on an operator who forgot to scan, the classic source of unexplained stock differences.

✓Fewer losses and less searching

Tagged equipment, tools and returnable packaging are located in seconds and stop disappearing unnoticed — small assets no longer evaporate between departments.

How an RFID system works

The reader emits a radio field. A passive tag takes the energy it needs from that field, answers with the identifier stored in the chip, and the reader passes it on to software. An active tag transmits by itself from its own battery, which is why it can be read from further away.

Software is the part that adds value: at UHF the identifier is an EPC code, and the stock system, ERP or WMS turns it into a stock movement. Example from a Romanian firm: the gate at goods-in reads the tags on a pallet and books it into stock before anyone fills in the goods-receipt note by hand. Without that integration the readers only produce a list of identifiers that nobody uses.

RFID frequencies: LF, HF (including NFC) and UHF

LF (around 125 kHz) is read from a few centimetres and appears in older access cards and key fobs (the EM4100 family) and in animal identification, where devices are certified to ISO 11784/11785 and ICAR keeps the register of conforming ones. HF, at 13.56 MHz, is the band of proximity cards and tickets (ISO/IEC 14443, ISO/IEC 15693).

NFC uses the same 13.56 MHz frequency, at a typical range of up to 2 cm according to the NFC Forum, and can read ISO/IEC 14443 A/B and 15693 cards. That is why a phone reads NFC tags but not UHF tags.

UHF is the stock-management band. Its air interface is defined by ISO/IEC 18000-63 (860–960 MHz) and by GS1 EPC Gen2, first published by EPCglobal in 2004; the current version is 3.0.1, ratified in February 2026, and the commercial name is RAIN RFID. In the EU, UHF readers work mainly in 865–868 MHz, up to 2 W e.r.p., under ETSI EN 302 208. Reading works over metres, and one pass can catch hundreds of tags. The band is chosen by range, environment (metal, liquids) and what the existing infrastructure already reads.

RFID, barcodes and NFC: the differences

A barcode needs direct line of sight and one scan per item; an RFID tag is read through packaging and in bulk. In return, an RFID tag costs more than a printed code, and the infrastructure (readers, an RFID label printer, integration) comes on top.

NFC is a subset of RFID at 13.56 MHz, with a range of centimetres and the phone as the reader: suited to payments, access and interactive tags, not to reading a whole pallet. A common solution in a warehouse is the combination: RFID on pallets and assets, barcodes at item level.

RFID card, RFID chip and RFID wallet protection

An RFID card is a card with a chip and antenna: the access, time-clock or transport card, and the contactless bank card, which works at 13.56 MHz. At electric-vehicle charging stations, the RFID card identifies the user's account.

"RFID protection" in a wallet is a metal foil or sleeve that blocks the field of a foreign reader. Contactless cards are read only from close up, from a few centimetres, so the risk is one of proximity, not of distance.

For a business: when it pays off and what it costs

On 18.11.2026, rfidshop.ro lists a self-adhesive UHF label (EPC Gen2, 80×25 mm) at 1.26 lei excluding VAT (1.53 lei including VAT) each, minimum 50 pieces; at large volumes the price is negotiated with the supplier. When tagging pallets and assets, the big cost is usually not the tag but the readers and the integration of the readings with the ERP or WMS; when tagging every single item, the volume of tags weighs heavily.

Metal reflects radio waves and liquids absorb them, so any project starts with a pilot on your real products in your real environment. The integration estimate comes after an analysis of your stock flow. RFID fits naturally with batch traceability: every movement read becomes a record.

Frequently asked questions

What is an RFID chip?

An RFID chip is the microcircuit that holds the unique identifier and, together with the antenna, forms the tag or card. It can be passive, powered by the reader, or active, with its own battery. Common families are EM4100 at 125 kHz, Mifare at 13.56 MHz and NXP UCODE at UHF.

What does an RFID reader do?

The reader emits the radio field, powers the passive tags in range, picks up their identifiers and sends them to software. It can be handheld, for stocktakes, or a fixed gate with antennas at goods-in and dispatch, where it records automatically what enters and leaves.

RFID or barcodes — how do I choose for my warehouse?

Choose barcodes for simple flows at modest volumes. Choose RFID when item-by-item scanning blocks the work: frequent stocktakes, bulk goods-in on pallets, assets that go missing. Many warehouses combine them, with RFID on pallets and barcodes at item level.

How much does an RFID tag cost?

On 18.11.2026, rfidshop.ro lists a self-adhesive UHF label at 1.26 lei excluding VAT (1.53 lei including VAT) each, minimum 50 pieces. The real cost includes readers, an RFID label printer and ERP integration, the part that is easiest to underestimate.

Does RFID work on metal or liquids?

Yes, with the right tags: metal reflects radio waves and liquids absorb them, which degrades reading of standard labels. Special on-metal tags and placement techniques solve the problem, which is why you start with a pilot on your real products.
