What is Emergency Ordinance 155/2024 (OUG / GEO 155/2024)?

OUG 155/2024 — Government Emergency Ordinance (GEO) 155/2024 in English sources — is the act through which Romania transposed the EU NIS2 directive, defining who carries cybersecurity obligations and who supervises them.

Government Emergency Ordinance no. 155 of 30 December 2024 (OUG in Romanian, GEO in English) is the act that turned the EU NIS2 directive into Romanian law, approved with amendments by Law no. 124/2025 and last amended by Law no. 123/2026. The text establishes the cybersecurity framework for Romania's civil national cyberspace and designates DNSC — the National Cybersecurity Directorate — as the competent authority: covered entities register with DNSC, significant incidents are reported to it, and the orders the Directorate issued in 2025 and 2026 detail the registration procedure and the risk-assessment methodology. The ordinance classifies regulated organizations into essential and important entities, across the sectors listed in its annexes — from energy, healthcare and drinking water to digital infrastructure and manufacturing — and generally targets medium-sized and large organizations; Article 9, however, allows coverage regardless of size in special situations, such as being the sole provider of an essential service. One detail frequently misrepresented in commercial pitches: in the public administration chapter, the ordinance covers central public administration entities, not every local institution. Entities that do fall in scope must notify DNSC within 30 days at most, under Article 18, and implement the risk-management measures the law requires.

Why it matters for your business

✓The text that settles arguments

When an offer or a cold email invokes "the NIS2 law", the ordinance is the document the claim is checked against: the sectors in the annexes, the size thresholds and the special coverage paths are written down, not negotiable.

✓A single counterpart: DNSC

Registration, incident reporting and supervision all run through one authority, and its published procedures fix the forms and the steps — a clear administrative route for covered entities.

✓Verifiable classification criteria

Sector, size, the special situations in Article 9 and direct designation form a finite checklist; an organization can establish in writing whether it has obligations instead of paying preemptively for compliance services.

The acts that make up Romania's NIS2 law

OUG 155/2024 was published in Monitorul Oficial no. 1332 of 31 December 2024 and repealed Law 362/2018, Romania's first NIS law (Article 66). The sanctions (Articles 60-61) apply 30 days after publication, under Article 64(4). Law 124/2025 (Monitorul Oficial no. 638 of 7 July 2025; the consolidated text applies it from 10 July 2025) approved the ordinance with amendments, including accredited training for management (Article 14(2)) and a rewritten fine scale (Article 60(2)). Law 123/2026 (Monitorul Oficial no. 550 of 3 July 2026; in the consolidated text from 6 July 2026) added to Article 36: DNSC, as coordinator of vulnerability disclosure, consults an advisory committee without legal personality made up of the authorities named in Law 58/2023.

DNSC orders detail the procedures: Order 1/2025 (notification for registration) and Order 2/2025 (disruption thresholds and the risk-level methodology), both of 11 August 2025, Monitorul Oficial no. 776 of 20 August 2025; Order 3/2025 of 27 November 2025 (supervision and control norms); Order 1/2026 of 6 August 2026, Monitorul Oficial no. 712 of 27 August 2026 (risk-management measures and the maturity self-assessment methodology). The text that applies is the consolidated form on legislatie.just.ro (current consolidation: 06.07.2026).

Essential or important: who falls under the ordinance

Annex 1 lists 11 sectors of high criticality and Annex 2 another 7 critical sectors, 18 in total. Size is set under Article 8 (medium or large enterprises under Law 346/2004). Regardless of size, Article 5(1) makes these essential: central public administration entities (Annex 1), entities identified under Article 9, critical entities, DNS service providers, qualified trust service providers and TLD registries. Large companies in Annex 1 sectors are also essential (Article 5(2)), as are medium-sized public electronic communications providers and managed security service providers (Article 5(3)-(4)).

Under Article 6(1), medium and large companies in Annex 1 or 2 that are not essential are important; so are, regardless of size and when not essential, public electronic communications providers, trust service providers and entities identified under Article 9 (Article 6(2)). One useful detail: the health sector in Annex 1 also lists wholesale and retail trade of pharmaceutical products (CAEN Rev. 3 divisions 4646 and 4773). The local administration point and the Article 9 paths are covered above.

From notification to the DNSC decision: the deadlines

The entity notifies DNSC within 30 days of the ordinance becoming applicable to it (Article 18(2)), sending identification and contact data, sector and subsector, member states where it provides services, public IP ranges and data proving its status under Article 5 or 6 (Article 18(3)). DNSC decides within 60 days for essential entities and 150 days for important ones, counted from the notification (Article 18(4)-(5)).

The other deadlines run from the decision: management designates the network and information systems security officer within 30 days of the decision being communicated (Article 14(3)); the risk-level assessment is due within 60 days of the decision (Article 18(6)); the maturity self-assessment within another 60 days (Article 18(7)). The self-assessment repeats every year and is endorsed by management (Article 12(4)); essential entities send a remediation plan within 30 days of it (Article 12(5)). The management body approves the measures, oversees them and is liable for breaches (Article 14(1)). In short: after the first notification, every deadline runs from the DNSC decision, not from the law.

Incident reporting and sanctions

A significant incident (Article 15(6)) causes severe operational disruption or financial loss to the entity, or considerable material or non-material damage to others. Article 15(7) requires, to the national CSIRT (DNSC): an early warning within 24 hours at most, an incident notification within 72 hours at most, an intermediate report on the CSIRT's request, and a final report at most one month after the 72-hour notification.

Under Article 60(2), important entities face fines from 5,000 lei up to EUR 7 million (lei equivalent) or 1.4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher; essential entities from 10,000 lei up to EUR 10 million or 2%. Other contraventions carry lower bands (Article 60(2)(c)-(d)): 1,000-300,000 lei for important and 1,500-500,000 lei for essential entities, e.g. late registration notification or a missing risk assessment (Article 60(1)(n)-(t)); domain-name registries and registrars, unauthorised CSIRT activity and auditors face 1,000-100,000 lei (Article 60(2)(e)); serious breaches of Article 50(2) carry 3,000-600,000 lei (Article 60(2^1)). DNSC's first fine, of 29 September 2026, was 50,000 lei against a body of the central public administration (sector 10, Annex 1) for late registration notification, Article 18(2) and Article 60(1)(o); DNSC announced it on 30 September 2026.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between NIS2 and OUG 155/2024?

NIS2 is the European directive — a framework each member state transposes into its own legislation. OUG 155/2024 is the Romanian transposition: it adopts the directive's architecture and concretely sets the competent authority, the registration procedure, the deadlines and the sanctions applicable in Romania. A Romanian organization reads its obligations from the ordinance and DNSC's orders, not from the directive directly.

Does OUG 155/2024 apply to city halls and local public institutions?

Not automatically. In the public administration chapter, the ordinance covers central public administration entities, according to Annex no. 1. A city hall or another local institution can fall in scope only through specific paths: if it operates services from the regulated sectors, if it meets the special criteria of Article 9, or if it is designated by DNSC — and the outcome of that check is worth documenting in writing.

What registration deadline does OUG 155/2024 set?

Entities that fall in scope must notify DNSC for registration within 30 days at most, under Article 18 — the term runs from the moment the law's provisions become applicable to that entity. DNSC then issues the registration decision within 60 days for essential and 150 days for important entities. That is followed by the stages set out in DNSC's procedures, including risk assessment and a maturity self-evaluation, with an ongoing duty to keep the submitted data current.

Is GEO 155/2024 the same as OUG 155/2024?

Yes. OUG is the Romanian abbreviation (Ordonanța de urgență a Guvernului) and GEO the English one (Government Emergency Ordinance). It is one act: no. 155 of 30 December 2024, published in Monitorul Oficial no. 1332, approved with amendments by Law 124/2025 and amended by Law 123/2026.

What are the incident reporting deadlines under OUG 155/2024?

For a significant incident, the entity sends DNSC's national CSIRT an early warning without undue delay and at most 24 hours after becoming aware, an incident notification within 72 hours, and a final report within one month of the 72-hour notification, under Article 15(7).

Has DNSC fined anyone under OUG 155/2024?

Yes. The first fine, decided on 29 September 2026 and announced on 30 September, was 50,000 lei against a specialised body of the central public administration, for late registration notification under Article 18(2) and Article 60(1)(o). It was the first sanction for failing the notification duty.
