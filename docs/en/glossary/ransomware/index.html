What is ransomware and how does an attack work?

Malware that encrypts or locks a company's data and demands money for the key; in today's attacks the data is also stolen, with a threat to publish it.

Ransomware (from ransom plus the -ware of software) is a type of malware that encrypts or locks a victim's files and systems and demands a ransom, usually in cryptocurrency, for the decryption key; in today's attacks the data is copied before encryption and the attackers threaten to publish it, a tactic called double extortion. According to the Verizon 2026 DBIR (published 19 May 2026), ransomware appears in 48% of the breaches analysed, up from 44% a year earlier.

The scene is almost always the same: on Monday morning nothing opens, files carry strange extensions, and a ransom note with a countdown fills the screen. What decides whether the attack is a bad day or a crisis is an offline backup proven by restore tests. The rest of this page shows how attackers get in, which legal duties follow and what is worth doing beforehand.

Why it matters for your business

The extortion defused in advance

A tested offline backup turns the attack's central threat into an inconvenience: you restore and refuse to negotiate, from a position of strength.

The favorite doors locked

2FA on remote access, current patches and EDR on workstations close the most frequent entry routes in the breaches Verizon analysed in 2026: unpatched vulnerabilities and stolen credentials.

Blast radius contained

Network segmentation and least-privilege rights keep one compromised computer from becoming the whole company encrypted overnight.

How a ransomware attack unfolds

A modern attack does not encrypt right away. The attacker gets in, explores the network for days or weeks, finds and destroys reachable backups, copies the data and only then encrypts the files and leaves the ransom note. CISA, the US cybersecurity agency, notes that many variants search for and delete or encrypt backups reachable from the network, and that internet-exposed remote services such as RDP are a common way in.

In the Verizon 2026 DBIR, the first way into small and medium businesses is exploitation of vulnerabilities (26% of breaches), followed by credential abuse (13%) and phishing (9%).

Ransomware itself comes in forms. Crypto ransomware makes files unreadable, while locker ransomware blocks access to the screen or system. Double extortion adds data theft, and ransomware-as-a-service means developers lease the program to "affiliates" who break into networks and share the profit (Microsoft Security 101). For malware types in general, see the Malware page.

Ransomware in numbers: 2026 and a Romanian case

Verizon 2026 DBIR: ransomware in 48% of breaches (44% the year before); 69% of victims did not pay; the median ransom paid was $139,875 ($150,000 the year before). The report notes that small businesses are disproportionately affected by ransomware.

In Romania, ANSPDCP announced on 17 November 2026 a fine of 26,649 lei (equivalent to 5,000 EUR) on a tourism operator (a hotel with SPA and wellness services) for breaching Article 32(1) and (2) GDPR. The investigation started from the operator's own notification under Article 33. The ransomware came in through a remote connection without multi-factor authentication, and the data affected included identification, contact, booking, billing, financial-accounting and user-account data.

The lesson: the fine was for missing security measures (above all MFA), not for the notification.

Your duties after an attack: GDPR and NIS2

If the attack affects personal data, Article 33(1) GDPR requires notifying ANSPDCP without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to individuals. Article 33(5) requires documenting every breach internally, and Article 34 requires informing the individuals concerned when the risk is high.

EDPB Guidelines 01/2021 (version 2.0, adopted 14 December 2021) cover ransomware in cases 01 to 04. With a working backup and no data exfiltrated, the attack may not require notification but must be documented (case 01). Without a backup, ANSPDCP is notified (case 02). With data exfiltrated and no backup, ANSPDCP is notified and the individuals are informed (case 04).

NIS2 applies only to the essential or important entities under GEO 155/2024, generally medium or large organisations in the sectors in its annexes. For a significant incident (Article 15(6)), they send DNSC an early warning within 24 hours, a notification within 72 hours and a final report within one month of the notification (Article 15(7)). Most small firms are outside NIS2, but GDPR still applies to them. The notification steps are on the data breach page.

Paying the ransom: what the facts show

Payment guarantees no recovery: decryptors can be faulty and data can come back partial. No More Ransom (an initiative of the Dutch police, Europol EC3, Kaspersky and McAfee) advises not to pay: payment confirms that ransomware works and does not guarantee the key. The site offers free decryptors for some families. In the 2026 DBIR, 69% of victims did not pay. The incident can be reported to DNSC.

The defense that works in a small company

Offline or immutable backup, proven by restore tests. The 3-2-1 rule (three copies, two media, one off site) is also cited by the EDPB in Guidelines 01/2021. A backup on a network-reachable drive can be encrypted or deleted along with everything else. Details are on the Backup and Disaster recovery pages.

Two-factor authentication on all remote access: the ANSPDCP case above started from its absence. Current patches, because vulnerabilities are the first way into SMBs (26%). EDR on workstations, for encryption and reconnaissance behavior. Network segmentation, so one compromised computer stays one computer. Staff trained against phishing.

Frequently asked questions

Where does the word ransomware come from?⌄

Ransomware comes from ransom plus -ware, as in software. It describes malware that holds data hostage, encrypted or locked, until a payment is made in exchange for the decryption key. The English term is also used in Romanian.

Is a small company a ransomware target?⌄

Yes: attacks start from automated scans for exposed services and stolen passwords, not from company size. The Verizon 2026 DBIR notes that small businesses are disproportionately affected by ransomware. At SMBs the first way in is an unpatched vulnerability, in 26% of breaches.

What should you do in the first hours after a ransomware attack?⌄

Disconnect the affected systems from the network and do not power them off if you can isolate them, because CISA warns that shutting down erases evidence in memory. Do not reinstall, check the state of the backups, call incident-response specialists and look for a decryptor on nomoreransom.org. Notification is covered on the data breach page.

Must a ransomware attack be notified to ANSPDCP?⌄

Yes, within 72 hours, if personal data was affected and a risk to individuals is not unlikely (Article 33 GDPR). With a working backup and no data stolen, the EDPB accepts internal documentation without notification. Entities under GEO 155/2024 also send, for a significant incident, an early warning to DNSC within 24 hours.

Is cloud sync a backup against ransomware?⌄

No: encrypted files sync too, overwriting the copies. You need a dedicated backup with independently kept versions, ideally immutable and proven by restore tests. The test question for your provider: if every workstation is encrypted and the attacker has our passwords, does the backup survive?
