What is waterfall?

Waterfall, the cascade model, organises a project in successive phases — requirements, design, build, testing, delivery — each finished before the next one begins.

Waterfall is the project methodology in which requirements, design, build, testing and release happen strictly one after another, each phase closed and approved before the next opens, with plan, budget and deadline fixed at the start. In Romanian it is called the cascade model. The classic diagram comes from Winston W. Royce's paper (TRW), "Managing the Development of Large Software Systems", IEEE WESCON, August 1970, where Royce writes that the strictly sequential implementation "is risky and invites failure"; the name "waterfall" appears in 1976 in Bell and Thayer, who credit it to Royce. Its practical opposite is agile: delivery in short iterations with continuous feedback. Royce's own context was software for spacecraft mission planning and command.

Its appeal is genuine: predictability on paper, since you know from the outset what you get, when and for how much, plus complete documentation and clear roles and responsibilities. Its structural risk is that feedback arrives at the end. The client sees working software only after months, when the inevitable discoveries (this is not what we pictured, the specification meant different things to different readers) cost the most, because everything is already built. For a buyer choosing today, the defensive question stays the same whatever the label on the quote: when do I first see something working, and how often after that?

Why it matters for your business

✓Predictability where requirements are certain

With genuinely fixed specifications such as a regulation, a defined integration or a migration, a complete plan with closed price and deadline is exactly right.

✓Documentation as a by-product

Signed phases naturally produce specifications, architecture and manuals, valuable at audit, for compliance work and when a project changes hands between teams.

✓Contracts that are simple to administer

Fixed deliverables, clear acceptance criteria and payments per phase form the model procurement departments understand without needing anything translated.

Phases of the waterfall model

The model reads as a list of phases, each closed by a clear output: 1. requirements, what the product must do, closed by a signed document; 2. design, the architecture and structure of the solution, closed by an approved technical specification; 3. build, writing the code to the design; 4. testing, checking against the requirements, closed when the acceptance criteria are met; 5. delivery, the release to the client; 6. operation and maintenance, the fixes and changes after release.

Textbook versions differ in the number of phases (five, six or seven), but the logic is the same: you do not start a phase until the one before it is closed. Figure 2 of Royce's paper has seven boxes: system requirements, software requirements, analysis, program design, coding, testing and operations.

What Royce actually wrote in 1970

Royce drew the sequential model but judged it risky and said so in the text: "I believe in this concept, but the implementation described above is risky and invites failure" (p. 2). The reason: testing is the first point where real behaviour in time, in memory and at input-output becomes visible. A problem found there can send the project back to design, with "up to a 100-percent overrun in schedule and/or costs". His context was software for spacecraft mission planning and command.

Royce proposed five additions: program design comes first, document the design, do it twice with a pilot version, plan, control and monitor testing, and involve the customer formally at several points before delivery. The word "waterfall" does not appear in his paper. Bell and Thayer (TRW, Proceedings of the 2nd International Conference on Software Engineering, 1976) write that he introduced the "waterfall" of development activities. Conclusion: the model taught as "Royce's waterfall" is the version Royce warned against.

Variants and alternatives

The V-model pairs each level of design with a level of testing so that verification starts earlier. The incremental model delivers the product in successive pieces, and the spiral model repeats cycles driven by risk analysis.

Agile is the set of values published in the Agile Manifesto (February 2001, Snowbird, Utah): delivery in short iterations with continuous feedback; details are on the agile page. Scrum is an agile framework with sprints of one month or less (Scrum Guide 2020); details are on the scrum page.

When waterfall makes sense for a company

Waterfall remains legitimate when requirements are stable and fully known (integration with a fixed tax specification, implementing a regulation, a well-defined migration), when regulation demands documentation per phase, or when hardware and software are built together and changing as we go does not exist.

For typical business software, where requirements are discovered through use, iterative delivery structurally reduces risk. The pragmatic hybrid is often the healthiest: framing phases (initial analysis, budget, architecture) followed by iterative build with regular demonstrations. The warning sign: any project of many months where the first working software appears at the end concentrates all the risk in the most expensive day.

Frequently asked questions

Waterfall or agile for our project — the short answer?

It turns on the certainty of the requirements. If you know them completely and they will not change — a regulation, a fixed technical specification, a defined migration — waterfall on that slice is legitimate. If you will discover them along the way, which is the near-universal case for business applications, iterative delivery with regular demonstrations is safer. The honesty test for knowing exactly what we want: have three people in the company describe the finished product separately, then compare the descriptions.

Fixed-price, fixed-deadline quotes look safer. Where is the catch?

In who pays for the discoveries. A fixed price on incomplete requirements is defended either through change requests, where every additional realisation becomes a billed amendment — the flexibility iterative delivery includes and this model sells separately — or through literal delivery of an imperfect specification, where you receive what you wrote rather than what you needed. The safety is real only as far as the specification is: on solid requirements a fixed price is fair, on guessed ones it is an illusion with a rate card attached.

Can we combine a fixed plan and budget with delivery in stages?

Yes, and that hybrid is the healthiest arrangement on the market: a sequential frame with serious initial analysis, architecture, an agreed total budget and horizon, containing iterative execution inside it — fortnightly demonstrations and priorities adjustable within the budget. You get the financial predictability approval requires and the continuous control that prevents a surprise at the end. Ask for it in the contract as fixed budget and objectives, content managed iteratively, working software demonstrated regularly.

What are the phases of the waterfall methodology?

Requirements, design, build, testing, delivery, and operation with maintenance, in that order. Each phase ends with an approved output, such as a signed document, a specification or met acceptance criteria, before the next begins. Textbooks differ on the exact number of phases, not on the order.

What does waterfall mean in Romanian?

"Cascadă". In IT, the cascade model means project phases flow in one direction, from requirements towards delivery, like water over steps: once a phase is closed you do not return to it except at the cost of a change.

Who invented the waterfall model?

The model is usually attributed to Winston Royce, who drew it in 1970 but called the pure version risky and proposed five corrections. The name "waterfall" appears in 1976 in Bell and Thayer, who credit Royce with the "waterfall" of development activities.
