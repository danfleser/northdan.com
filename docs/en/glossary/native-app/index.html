What is a native app?

An app written specifically for one operating system, using its official language and tools: Swift for iPhone, Kotlin for Android.

A native app is a program written specifically for one operating system, using that system's official language and tools — Swift and Xcode for iPhone and iPad, Kotlin and Android Studio for Android — and installed from the App Store or Google Play. Google announced at Google I/O 2019 that Android development would become Kotlin-first, and Apple presents Swift as the language for all its platforms; older code may be in Objective-C (iOS) or Java (Android). Because it talks directly to the system, a native app has full access to the camera, GPS, Bluetooth, NFC, biometrics and notifications, and follows the platform's design rules: the Human Interface Guidelines at Apple, Material Design at Google.

The price is duplicated effort: for both systems you have two separate apps with two codebases, and every feature is written and tested twice, at launch and at every update. The alternative is a single codebase: cross-platform (Flutter, React Native), with an indicative saving of 30–40% against two native apps, or hybrid (web code inside a WebView, with Ionic or Capacitor). The full comparison, with decision criteria, is on the native vs hybrid page. For your company the decision is economic rather than ideological: native pays off when performance or hardware is the product (augmented reality, real-time video, industrial or medical Bluetooth, watch apps), not for ordering, booking or a catalogue.

Why it matters for your business

✓The highest performance available

The code talks directly to the operating system, so intensive graphics, real-time processing and complex animation run with no intermediate layer.

✓Day-one access to new platform features

New capabilities in iOS and Android are usable immediately in native apps, well before they filter through into cross-platform frameworks.

✓Seamless platform integration

Widgets, Apple Pay and Google Pay, system extensions — the entire platform ecosystem is available without intermediate layers or plugin waits.

How a native app is built: languages and tools

On iPhone and iPad the language is Swift (Objective-C appears in older code) and the tool is Xcode, which runs only on a Mac and is distributed through the Mac App Store. Interfaces are written with SwiftUI or UIKit. On Android the language is Kotlin (Java in older code) and the tool is Android Studio, with Jetpack Compose for the interface, the toolkit Android recommends for native UI.

To publish you need an Apple Developer account (99 USD a year) and a Google Play registration (25 USD, once). Native desktop apps for Windows or macOS exist as well, but are not the subject of this page.

What it adds: hardware and system integration

Native code has direct access to the camera, GPS, Bluetooth and NFC, Face ID or fingerprint, push notifications, widgets, Apple Pay and Google Pay, and can work fully offline. New system APIs are available from the day the system ships; cross-platform frameworks usually expose them later.

The Human Interface Guidelines and Material Design make gestures and menus behave the way an iPhone or Android user expects.

Examples and when to choose native

The apps preinstalled on a phone, such as Camera, Settings or Phone, are native. Projects that choose native include augmented reality (ARKit and ARCore), real-time video or audio processing, Bluetooth connections to medical or industrial devices, and watch apps.

For an ordinary business flow with ordering, booking or a catalogue, the balance of cost and benefit usually favours a single codebase.

Maintenance: the updates Apple and Google require

Store rules change every year. Since 28 April 2026, apps uploaded to App Store Connect must be built with Xcode 26 or later, using the iOS 26 SDK (or iPadOS, tvOS, visionOS, watchOS 26). From 31 August 2026, new apps and updates on Google Play must target Android 16 (API 36), and existing apps must target at least Android 15 (API 35) to stay available to new users on devices running newer Android versions.

The rules apply to every app in the stores, not only native ones, but with native you meet them twice, in two codebases. Maintenance costs an indicative 15–20% a year of the development cost.

Frequently asked questions

When does a business app justify native development?

When performance or hardware is the essence of the product: video processing, augmented reality, connections to specialised devices — or when user volumes make every optimisation commercially valuable. For classic business workflows, rarely.

Can we launch natively on one platform first?

Yes, and it is a legitimate strategy. You launch on whichever platform dominates your audience and validate the product before doubling the investment. Which platform that is varies by market: in Romania, for example, Android had 73.8% of mobile usage and iOS 26.2% in October 2026 (StatCounter), but check your own analytics.

Can the team that builds our website also write native apps?

Not automatically. Swift and Kotlin are separate specialisations from web development, and many agencies cover native work through partners. Ask explicitly who writes the code and who maintains it after launch — the answer often differs.

Which languages are used for native apps?

Swift (and Objective-C in older code) for iOS, Kotlin (and Java) for Android. Google has called Android development Kotlin-first since 2019. iOS apps are built in Xcode on a Mac, Android apps in Android Studio.

How much does a native app cost?

As a guide, a mobile MVP with its own backend costs 6,000–18,000 €. Native sits at the top of the MVP band: 9,000–18,000 € for Android and 10,000–18,000 € for iOS, and covering both platforms means two apps. A business app with integrations (roles, payments, admin panel) costs about 18,000–50,000 €, up to 55,000 € on iOS. Cross-platform brings an indicative saving of 30–40% compared with two native apps.

How can you tell whether an app is native?

You cannot be sure from simply using it: Flutter or React Native can look identical to a native app. It shows in the app package (APK or IPA), where Flutter and React Native leave their own libraries, or in the source code. If you are commissioning one, ask for the language (Swift or Kotlin), access to the code repository and who maintains it after launch in the offer and the contract.
