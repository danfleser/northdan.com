What is usability?

Usability shows how well users of a product complete their task: correctly, without wasted effort and satisfied.

6

6

Usability, according to ISO 9241-11:2018, is the extent to which a system, product or service can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use. It is an outcome of use, measured on real people doing real tasks, not an opinion about how the design looks. It is narrower than UX, which covers the whole experience before, during and after use, and it differs from accessibility, which asks whether everybody can use the product, including people with disabilities.

For a company, usability shows up in numbers: abandoned order flows, support calls born from confusion, internal applications bypassed with spreadsheets. There are two good moments to test. The first is before development, on a prototype, when a correction costs hours. The second is periodically on the live product: every product in use has a current top three frustrations worth knowing and repairing, and one that does not know them is not free of frustration, only of measurement.

Why it matters for your business

✓Real problems discovered cheaply

A test with five users produces, in a single afternoon, the list of obstacles that have been driving customers away for months, as a concrete list ranked by impact.

✓Frequent tasks optimised to the second

Steps removed from daily operations multiply with every use, which makes usability pure productivity in internal applications people open dozens of times a day.

✓Errors prevented rather than merely handled

Design that makes a mistake hard to commit — confirmation on destructive actions, timely validation, clear messages — cuts both frustration and its support costs.

The ISO 9241-11 definition: effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction

The standard defines three measures. Effectiveness is the accuracy and completeness with which users achieve their goals (ISO 9241-11:2018, 3.1.12). Efficiency is the resources spent relative to the result: time, steps, effort. Satisfaction is the extent to which the user's physical, cognitive and emotional responses meet their needs and expectations (3.1.14).

Context of use matters too: the users, their goals and tasks, the resources and the environment. The same site can be usable for a returning customer and unusable for a first-time visitor on a phone. The second edition (2018) replaced the 1998 edition, and ISO reconfirmed it in 2023, so it is the version in force.

In practice each dimension is measured: task completion rate, number of errors, time and steps per task, and a short satisfaction questionnaire after the test.

Jakob Nielsen's five criteria

Nielsen Norman Group ("Usability 101", Jakob Nielsen, 2012; rewritten by Raluca Budiu on 6 November 2026 with the same components) describes usability through five criteria: learnability, efficiency, memorability, errors (how many are made, how severe, and how easily people recover) and satisfaction.

The list has five items, not six. NN/g adds that a product is valuable only with both utility (the features that are needed) and usability (how easy they are to use).

The usability test with 5 users

Jakob Nielsen ("Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users", NN/g, 18 March 2000) models the number of problems found as N(1 − (1 − L)ⁿ), where L averages 31% per user across the projects studied. It follows that 5 users find about 85% of the problems. Three rounds of 5 with repairs in between are more useful than one round of 15. For distinct user groups, test 3–4 people from each group.

Quantitative studies, with numbers and comparisons, need around 20 users, NN/g recommends. A test can be moderated (a facilitator runs the session and observes) or unmoderated (the user works alone), with the participant thinking aloud and tasks phrased as goals. The concrete steps are in the questions below.

Heuristic evaluation

Usability can also be assessed by a specialist who runs the product through established principles. A heuristic evaluation is fast and cheap, complements testing with people and does not replace it. Nielsen's 10 heuristics (formulated with Rolf Molich in 1990, refined in 1994; NN/g, "10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design") include visibility of system status, consistency and standards, error prevention, recognition rather than recall, and help in recognising and recovering from errors.

Frequently asked questions

How do we actually run a usability test for a company website?

Without a laboratory: recruit five people from the target audience (existing customers or acquaintances who fit the profile, never colleagues who know the product), give them four or five tasks phrased as goals ("find out what X costs"), ask them to think aloud, then stay quiet without guiding or defending the product. Note every hesitation. Remote works too, over a video call with a shared screen.

Usability and accessibility — what is the difference and which do we need?

Usability optimises the experience of the typical user: easy, fast, unfrustrating. Accessibility guarantees that everybody can use the product, including people with visual, hearing or mobility impairments. Neither substitutes for the other: a usable site can be inaccessible and the reverse holds too. On the legal side, the European Accessibility Act (Directive (EU) 2019/882) applies from 28 June 2025 to certain products and services, with exemptions for microenterprises. See WCAG in the glossary.

Our product launched years ago — is usability testing still worth it?

All the more so. A live product has data, since analytics shows where flows are abandoned while testing explains why, and it has real users who are easy to recruit. The effect of repairs can be measured directly in analytics, on the corrected flows. A healthy rhythm is one test at every major change plus an annual pass over the main flows, because products, audiences and expectations change over time.

Usability and UX — what is the difference?

Usability is the outcome of use, measured by effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction (ISO 9241-11). UX is the user's perceptions and responses before, during and after use (ISO 9241-210:2019, 3.15), so it is broader and includes brand, expectations and trust. Usability is a measurable part of UX.

How many users do we need for a usability test?

To find problems, 5 per round (about 85% of problems, Nielsen, 2000), repeated after repairs. For distinct user groups, 3–4 from each group. For quantitative studies, with numbers and comparisons, NN/g recommends around 20 users.

How do you measure the usability of a website?

Through task completion rate, time and steps per task, number of errors and the score of a satisfaction questionnaire after the test. Analytics complements this: it shows where flows are abandoned, and the test with people says why.
