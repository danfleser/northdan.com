IT Glossary

What is artificial intelligence (AI)?

Programs that learn from data instead of being given every rule by a person: they understand language, recognise images, make predictions and generate content.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the branch of computer science that builds programs able to perform tasks once thought to need human intelligence: they learn from data, recognise language and images, make predictions, generate content and take decisions.

In European Union law, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the AI Act), Article 3(1), defines an “AI system” as a machine-based system designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy that, from the input it receives, infers how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments. Recital 12 excludes systems based on rules defined solely by natural persons: a rule such as “if an invoice goes over 10,000 lei, send it to the director” is automation, not AI. In Romania the abbreviation used in official texts is IA, which is why Romanian sources write IA and AI interchangeably.

Under the AI label live different capabilities: generating text and images, understanding documents (scanned invoices become data), conversation, prediction on historical data (which clients leave, which stock runs out) and visual recognition (defects on a line, products on a shelf). For a small company the right question is not “what can AI do?” but “where does it hurt me, repetitively and expensively?”: that is where a use case is found. The rules that stay valid: AI errs with confidence, so a person validates anything with real consequences; input quality dictates output quality; cost is measured per case; and data-protection law applies, meaning you know what data goes to which service. Start with a small, measurable pilot, not a strategy document.

Why it matters for your business

Repetitive hours returned to the firm

Correspondence, data extraction from documents, first drafts of quotes and content: tasks that ate days compress into minutes, with human review.

Answers from company documents, at any hour

A chatbot that searches the company's manuals, procedures and frequent questions (RAG) answers clients and colleagues outside office hours too, pointing to the document it took the answer from.

Data-supported decisions

Models trained on your own history (demand, churn, stock) give an estimate with a probability that the manager sets beside their own experience.

How artificial intelligence works

An AI system goes through three stages. First comes training data: thousands or millions of examples, such as texts, images, invoices or transactions. Then a learning algorithm builds a model, meaning the patterns deduced from those examples. Finally, in use (called inference), new data enters the model and a result comes out: a prediction, a text, a classification.

The difference from classical programming: there a person writes the rules, here the model deduces them from examples. Picture three years of invoices, some paid on time, some late. A model learns what the late ones have in common (customer, amount, month) and, for a new invoice, estimates the risk of late payment. The result is a probability, not a certainty, so a person decides what to do with it.

Types of artificial intelligence

By capability: the systems used in companies today, ChatGPT included, are specialised systems, also called narrow AI: each is built for a category of tasks, such as text, images or predictions. General AI (AGI), a system that could do any intellectual task a human can, and superintelligence are research goals and hypotheses, not product categories on the market.

By technique: machine learning is learning from examples; deep learning is machine learning on neural networks with many layers; large language models (LLMs) are networks of this kind trained on text, and generative AI is the family that produces new content (text, images, code). By application area: natural language processing, computer vision, speech recognition and recommendation systems. Most have their own page in this glossary.

Artificial intelligence, machine learning and automation

Machine learning (ML) is the subfield of AI in which the program learns from data. AI is broader: recital 12 of the AI Act names, besides machine learning, logic- and knowledge-based approaches. So “AI means only machine learning” is also a simplification.

Classical automation, meaning rules written by people that run the same way every time (a robot that copies data between two programs, an alert when stock falls under a threshold), is not AI according to the same recital. It matters in practice: automation is predictable and cheap to verify, AI adapts to new cases but can be wrong, so it needs control. Many good processes use both.

What the law says: the AI Act and its 2024–2028 dates

1 August 2024: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 enters into force (Article 113). 2 February 2025: Chapters I and II apply, meaning the definitions, AI literacy (Article 4) and the prohibited practices (Article 5). 2 August 2025: general-purpose AI models (Chapter V), governance and penalties (Chapter XII).

2 August 2026: general application, including the transparency rules of Article 50; for example, a chatbot must be designed so that people learn they are talking to AI (Article 50(1), a provider obligation).

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (the Digital Omnibus on AI), published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and in force from 27 July 2026, reworded Article 4: providers and deployers take measures to support the AI literacy of their staff, without guaranteeing any particular level of knowledge. It postponed the obligations for high-risk systems to 2 December 2027 (Annex III: recruitment, credit scoring, education) and 2 August 2028 (Annex I: AI in regulated products) and added new prohibitions, applicable from 2 December 2026.

In Romania, the national law setting the duties and penalties of the supervisory authority was still being drafted on 18 November 2026, according to ANCOM's AI page; the regulation applies directly all the same. Fines and authorities are explained on the AI Act page.

Where a small company uses artificial intelligence

The cases that work in practice: correspondence and quoting, extracting data from documents (invoices, contracts), a chatbot that searches the company knowledge base, summarising and searching internal archives, help with programming and marketing, forecasting demand or stock, and visual inspection of products.

The limits are just as practical. Generative models can state false things with complete confidence (hallucinations), so a person validates everything that goes to a client. Data and documents must be put in order first, otherwise the result is weak. And you need to know which data reaches an external service, because GDPR stays in force. Pick one measurable case, run it for a few weeks and compare with the before state; the step-by-step guide is on the page about how a small company starts using AI.

Frequently asked questions

What does AI stand for, and why do EU texts in Romanian say IA?⌄

AI stands for artificial intelligence: programs that learn from data and produce predictions, content, recommendations or decisions. In Romanian the abbreviation is IA (inteligență artificială), the form used in official EU texts in Romanian, including the AI Act. AI is the English form; both mean the same thing.

What is the difference between artificial intelligence and machine learning?⌄

Machine learning is a part of artificial intelligence: the technique by which a program learns from examples instead of rules written by a person. AI also includes logic- and knowledge-based systems (recital 12 of the AI Act). Deep learning is machine learning on neural networks, and LLMs are deep learning applied to text.

Is ChatGPT artificial intelligence?⌄

Yes, ChatGPT is generative artificial intelligence: a large language model (LLM), specialised in text and conversation. It predicts the continuation of a text from learned patterns, which is why it can be confidently wrong. It is a system specialised in one category of tasks, not a general intelligence.

What duties does a company that only uses AI have in 2026?⌄

A company that uses an AI system in its own activity is a deployer (Article 3(4) of the AI Act). It takes AI-literacy measures for staff (Article 4, reworded by Regulation 2026/1744) and, from 2 August 2026, discloses published deepfakes (Article 50(4)). The heavy duties concern high-risk systems, from 2 December 2027. This is not legal advice.

What are AI “hallucinations”, and how serious are they?⌄

Generative models can state false things with confidence: they invent figures, sources, clauses. Severity depends on context: in brainstorming it costs nothing, in a quote sent to a client it is an incident. The standard rule: AI proposes, a person validates. Techniques that ground answers in company documents (RAG) reduce the problem without eliminating it.

What AI project should a small company start with?⌄

A small, painful, measurable one: extracting data from incoming invoices, a chatbot on real frequent questions, or drafting replies to customers. A good pilot lasts a few weeks, has a capped cost and a result comparable with the before state; only on that evidence does it expand.
