Pricing guide

How much digitalizing a small company costs, in three levels

Three levels, three budgets, plus the costs that remain after launch — and what you can pay for by subscription only.

Digitalizing a small company costs, in 2026, an indicative €1,000–5,000 excluding VAT for online presence (website, business listing, booking or quote request), €5,000–25,000 for internal processes (electronic invoicing, customer records, stock management, connected to each other) and over €25,000 for integrated flows, where orders, stock, invoicing and accounting work on the same data. The price moves with how many systems you connect and whether you rent a ready-made product or build on the company's own flows: an application built on your own flows starts at €5,000, not at one or two thousand. Below level 1 there is also the subscription entry point: invoicing from €5.84/month + VAT, a CRM at €0–100 per user per month.

A workshop with 8 employees keeping orders in a notebook and invoices in a spreadsheet does not need the same digitalization as a distributor running three warehouses, which is why sensible budgets are discussed in levels rather than as one lump sum. On top of the project come the costs that remain after launch (maintenance, hosting, subscriptions), detailed below. Checked on 18 November 2026: the invoicing subscription price on the provider's page, the status of funding calls on the official pages.

Key takeaways

Level 1 — online presence: €1,000–5,000

A brochure website, a business listing, a contact or booking form. For many small companies this is the only level that is genuinely urgent.

Level 2 — internal processes: €5,000–25,000

Electronic invoicing, a simple CRM, stock management, job tracking. This is where the notebooks, the parallel spreadsheets and the knowledge held in one person's head disappear.

Level 3 — integrated flows: over €25,000

Orders, stock, invoicing and reporting connected into a single flow. It only makes sense once the workload means level 2 no longer suffices.

Monthly costs, separate from the project

Maintaining a brochure website costs €50–150/month; for custom-built software, 15–20% a year of the development cost. Put them in the budget from day one.

What each level includes and what it does not

Level 1 includes the website, the business listing and the contact or booking form. It does not include texts written by us (€30–80 per page) or hosting and the domain, which are recurring costs.

At level 2, the budget adds up from parts: implementing a subscription CRM €1,000–5,000, an e-Invoice module in your existing invoicing tool €0–500, implementing an off-the-shelf ERP €5,000–20,000. A custom CRM starts at a core of €5,000–8,000; the full version, €8,000–40,000, can push a project into level 3. Per-user licences, paid monthly, are not included, nor is CRM data migration, €500–2,000 separately.

Level 3 adds up several API integrations between systems (€3,000–15,000 each) or a modular custom ERP (€15,000–60,000). All sums are indicative and exclude VAT.

The costs that remain after launch

Maintaining a brochure website costs €50–150/month, an online store €150–500/month. Custom software needs 15–20% a year of the development cost, and automations 10–15% a year, because you are paying for adaptation to changes in the connected applications.

For recurring e-Invoice work count €20–100/month. Shared hosting costs 100–400 lei/year and a .ro domain 40–70 lei/year. With subscriptions, every extra person is paid monthly.

The thresholds that change the price

Each connected system adds an indicative €1,000–5,000 to a web application. Migrating old data means 10–20% of the project. On a subscription, every extra paying user enters the monthly bill, and an atypical sales or work process pushes the price toward the top of the level's band.

Level 2 stops being enough when several teams enter the same data in different systems, or when orders, stock and invoicing must update each other without human hands. Level 3 makes sense then, not before.

Where to start so the money is not wasted

The classic mistake is jumping straight to level 3 on the grounds that if you are doing it at all you may as well do everything. A small company gets the best ratio between investment and effect from the level that solves its biggest pain: if you lose customers because nobody finds you online, start with the website; if you lose hours on invoices and quotes, start with the internal processes. Digitalization done in the wrong order produces software nobody uses.

A simple prioritisation criterion: count the hours per week lost on each manual activity and multiply them by the hourly cost of the person doing them. The activity with the biggest total gets digitalized first. Done honestly, that calculation shows what is worth digitalizing first — and it also tells you when NOT to bother: if the total is small, keep the spreadsheet.

How to read a quote before you sign

The usual advance is 20–30%. A 50% advance before any code is written moves all the risk to the client, so ask for payment in stages. If you are unsure what you need, an SME systems audit (€1,000–3,000) or a full specification (€800–2,500) are paid deliverables that stay yours, whoever builds afterwards.

Ask for written answers to three questions: who owns the accounts, the code and the data; in what format and through what API you can export them; and how much of the price is licence versus custom build.

Funding: what is open on 18 November 2026

On 18 November 2026 the national PNRR programme for SME digitalization no longer accepts projects: applications were taken only between 15.02 and 30.06.2023. Two routes are open now. SME Eco-Tech, a national programme open to firms in every region (Swiss-Romanian, not EU money), until 30.12.2026, 20:00: a grant of at most 40%, capped at 267,240 lei, with a bank credit. And Intervention 1.4.3 of the Centru Regional Programme, only for SMEs in the ITI zones, until 16.12.2026, 12:00. From 02.12.2026, call 2 of Action 2.2 in the Centru Regional Programme joins them: submission until 02.02.2027, 12:00, €15,000–200,000 per project, up to 90% of eligible value, only for SMEs in the Centru region outside the ICT sector. For that call, payment runs through pre-financing (tranches of at most 10% of the contract's eligible value), a payment request or a reimbursement request; the minimum 10% own contribution stays with the firm.

The trap is letting the funding dictate the project: companies that bought complex ERP systems purely because they were eligible ended up with unused licences once the monitoring period expired. The healthy order is the reverse — first define which problems you are solving, then check which part of the solution fits the programme. That way funding accelerates digitalization instead of distorting it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the cheapest way to digitalize a small company?⌄

Subscriptions: invoicing from €5.84/month + VAT and a CRM at €0–100 per user per month. You pay little, but the company's process has to fit the product, and data export depends on the vendor.

What should a company with 5–10 employees allocate?⌄

Indicatively €1,000–5,000 for online presence, plus €5,000–25,000 for the processes that hurt most. Small companies usually stay in the lower part of level 2; larger sums only make sense when volume justifies integrated flows.

Do the prices include VAT, and how is payment made?⌄

No, all sums exclude VAT. The usual advance is 20–30%, the rest paid in delivered stages. A 50% advance before any code is written moves all the risk to the client.

How long does digitalizing a small company take?⌄

The first visible results usually appear within 1–2 months, most often from invoicing or order intake. A full programme, with stock management, reporting and linked flows, typically stretches over 6–18 months, at the team's pace.

Can I digitalize the company gradually, in pieces?⌄

Yes, and it is usually recommended: start with the level that hurts most, measure the effect, then extend. The technical condition is that every tool you choose can export its data and offers an API, so that two years from now you are not left with systems that cannot talk to each other.

Does digitalization mean I must abandon spreadsheets?⌄

No. Spreadsheets remain perfectly good for one-off analysis and small volumes. The problem appears when the same file is filled in by several people, tracks money or stock and becomes the single source of truth — that is where a system with access rights and an audit trail prevents costly errors.
